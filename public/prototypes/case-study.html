UI / UX case study · 2025

m8s

A roommate chore tracker that turns "whose turn is it?" into a fair rotation the whole apartment can see.

Role
Product design, front-end
Timeline
Sep – Nov 2025
Team
Oasis @ Northeastern, group 19
Tools
Figma, React, TypeScript
View repo ↗ Live site ↗
Problem

Shared apartments run on memory and group chats. Chores slip, the same people pick up the slack, and nobody has the full picture.

Approach

Rotate recurring chores automatically and make the week visible at a glance, so reminders come from the app, not a roommate.

Outcome

A full-stack web app with households, rotations and swaps, designed in Figma and built in React over one semester.

01

Context

m8s started as a group project for the Oasis project series at Northeastern. Our brief was open: build something full-stack that solves a problem we actually have. Four of us lived with roommates, and all four of us had the same complaint.

Chore charts on the fridge go stale in a week. Group chats bury the reminder under memes. The result is a quiet ledger of resentment that nobody wants to bring up.

Problem statement

How might we make sharing chores feel fair, without turning anyone into the household manager?

02

Research

We interviewed roommates across a handful of households and asked them to walk us through the last chore argument they had. The pattern was rarely about the chore itself.

12Interviews
5Households
3Test rounds

I don't mind doing the dishes. I mind being the only one who remembers they exist.

Roommate, interview 03

Fairness turned out to be about visibility: people were fine with uneven weeks as long as everyone could see the balance evening out over time.

03

Define

Four insights shaped every screen that followed.

Insight 01

Fairness is perceived

People judged fairness by effort and visibility, not by raw task count.

Insight 02

Nudges feel personal

A reminder from a roommate reads as nagging. The same reminder from the app reads as neutral.

Insight 03

Schedules drift

Rotations broke the first week someone travelled, so swapping had to be a first-class action.

Insight 04

Glanceable wins

Most check-ins lasted seconds. Home had to answer "what's mine today?" instantly.

04

Design

We went from paper to Figma in a week, then iterated on the chore board until a first-time user could find their task without instructions.

From a list to a rotation board

The first build was a to-do list. Testers read it as "my list," and the shared picture vanished. Rotating the layout to a household board, with each person as a column, fixed that in one round.

05

Outcome

We shipped a working app by the end of the series: accounts, households, rotating chores and swaps, all backed by a real database.

  • Full-stack app with auth, households and automatic rotations
  • Swap requests as a first-class action, not a workaround
  • A Figma component library mirrored one-to-one in React

06

Reflection

What worked
  • Solving the rotation logic on paper before touching the UI
  • Testing the board layout before polishing anything
Next time
  • Test with real households for longer than a week
  • Push notifications instead of in-app reminders only
